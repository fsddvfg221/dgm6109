Putters

The putter is the club you use most often in a round of golf. It is designed to roll the ball smoothly across the green and into the hole. This page looks at how putters are used, the different shapes and balance types, and what to consider when choosing one that fits your stroke.

When Do You Use a Putter?

Putters are used on the green, and sometimes just off the green, when you want to roll the ball rather than chip it into the air. Because putting can easily account for 30–40 strokes per round, your putter has a huge influence on your score.

A good putter, matched to your stroke and your eye, can:

  • help you start the ball on your intended line
  • control speed and distance more consistently
  • build confidence on short putts and long lag putts

Even small improvements with your putter can have a big impact on your overall performance.

Blade vs. Mallet Putters

The two main visual categories of putters are blade and mallet designs. Each style offers a different look, weight distribution, and level of forgiveness.

Blade Putters

Blades have a traditional, narrow head shape. They tend to be lighter in the head and are often preferred by players who like a more classic look and more feedback in their hands.

Many blade putters suit strokes with more arc, where the putter swings slightly inside on the backstroke and follow-through.

Mallet Putters

Mallet putters have larger heads with more weight distributed around the perimeter. They often include strong alignment lines or shapes on top.

Mallets are usually more forgiving on off-center hits and can suit players who prefer a straighter, more “straight back, straight through” putting stroke.

Face Balance and Toe Hang

Beyond head shape, the way a putter is balanced can influence how it wants to move during the stroke.

Face-Balanced Putters

When you balance a face-balanced putter on your finger, the face points up toward the sky. This design resists twisting and can work well for players with a straighter putting stroke that does not rotate much around the body.

Toe-Hang Putters

In a toe-hang putter, the toe of the club points downward when balanced. This type of putter allows more natural rotation of the face and can suit players with an arcing stroke.

Matching face balance or toe hang to your putting stroke can help the putter work with your motion instead of against it.

Insert vs. Milled Faces

The feel of a putter also depends on the face design and the materials used.

Face Inserts

Many putters use soft inserts made from polymer or other materials. These can give a softer, quieter feel at impact and can help off-center hits feel more consistent across the face.

Fully Milled Faces

Milled putters are created from a solid block of metal. The face often has a milled pattern to control sound and feel. These designs are known for a precise, solid feel and are popular with golfers who like clear feedback.

Length, Grip, and Alignment

A putter is more than just the head. Length, grip style, and alignment features also play big roles.

  • Length: Common lengths are around 33–35 inches. The right length helps you set up with your eyes comfortably over or just inside the ball.
  • Grip Size: Thicker grips can reduce wrist action, while thinner grips offer more hand feel.
  • Alignment Aids: Lines, dots, and shapes on the top of the putter help you aim more accurately.

Trying different combinations can help you find a setup that feels natural and repeatable.

Choosing a Putter for Your Stroke

There is no single perfect putter for everyone. The right model depends on:

  • how much arc you have in your putting stroke
  • whether you prefer a blade or mallet shape at address
  • how much feedback you want in your hands
  • whether you like a softer or firmer feel at impact

As an amateur golfer who often experiments with equipment, it is helpful to pay attention not just to made or missed putts, but to how confidently you set up and how consistent your roll looks on different greens.

Building Confidence on the Green

Putting is as much about confidence as it is about mechanics. A putter that fits your eye, your stroke, and your feel preferences can make it easier to trust your line and focus on speed.

Over time, finding a putter that feels like an extension of your hands can be one of the most valuable equipment choices you make as a golfer.
